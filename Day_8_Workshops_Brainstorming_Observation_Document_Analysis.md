# 1. Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

-   Explain when workshops, brainstorming, observation and document
    analysis are useful elicitation techniques.
-   Plan and facilitate a stakeholder workshop with a clear objective,
    agenda and expected outcome.
-   Explain the difference between brainstorming and a decision-making
    meeting.
-   Apply basic brainstorming rules and organize ideas into meaningful
    themes.
-   Observe a real business process and record what actually happens
    without unnecessarily interrupting the work.
-   Identify useful documents and analyze them for rules, data, roles,
    process steps and exceptions.
-   Compare information gathered from different sources and identify
    gaps or contradictions.
-   Record findings, assumptions and follow-up questions clearly.

# 2. Why These Techniques Matter

Imagine a college is planning an online admission portal.

The BA has already interviewed an admissions manager and two admissions
officers. Their answers explain the official process, but several
questions remain:

-   Do all staff follow the same process?
-   What happens during busy admission periods?
-   Are there unofficial spreadsheets used to track applications?
-   Which rules are written in the admission policy?
-   Do students and staff understand the process in the same way?
-   Which problems have not been mentioned in interviews?

A single interview may not reveal the complete picture. The BA can use
several techniques:

-   **Workshop:** Bring relevant stakeholders together to discuss and
    agree on a shared understanding.
-   **Brainstorming:** Generate possible causes or improvement ideas
    without judging them too early.
-   **Observation:** Watch staff perform the actual process.
-   **Document analysis:** Review admission forms, policies,
    spreadsheets, reports and existing system screens.

Each technique reveals a different kind of information. Combining them
helps the BA understand both the documented process and the way work is
actually performed.

**Main idea:** Elicitation techniques are methods for discovering
information. The BA chooses a technique based on the information needed,
the stakeholders involved, the time available and the complexity of the
topic.

# 4. Workshops

## 4.1 What is a workshop?

A workshop is a planned, collaborative session in which a group of
stakeholders work together to understand a topic, explore problems,
discuss requirements or reach a shared understanding.

A workshop is more than an ordinary meeting. It has: - A clear
purpose. - Relevant participants. - A planned agenda. - A facilitator. -
Activities that encourage participation. - Recorded outcomes and
actions.

**Example:** The BA organizes a workshop with admissions, finance,
student support and IT representatives to understand the end-to-end
admission process.

## 4.2 When should a BA use a workshop?

Workshops are useful when:

-   Several stakeholder groups are involved.
-   Different departments have different views of the same process.
-   The BA needs a shared understanding.
-   Requirements depend on decisions from multiple people.
-   Conflicting needs must be discussed.
-   A process or set of requirements needs to be reviewed together.
-   The team needs to generate and organize ideas.

A workshop may be less suitable when: - The topic is highly confidential
and cannot be discussed in a group. - One participant may dominate and
prevent others from contributing. - Key participants cannot attend. -
The objective is only to obtain a small amount of factual information
from one specialist.

In such cases, individual interviews may be more appropriate.

## 4.3 Who should attend?

Invite people who can contribute relevant knowledge or decisions.

For an admission portal:

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Participant                         Contribution
  ----------------------------------- -----------------------------------
  Admissions manager                  Explains process ownership and
                                      approval rules

  Admissions officer                  Explains daily activities and
                                      exceptions

  Finance representative              Explains application fees, payment
                                      confirmation and refunds

  Student support representative      Explains student questions and
                                      complaints

  IT representative                   Explains current system
                                      capabilities and technical
                                      constraints

  Student representative              Explains the applicant's experience

  BA / facilitator                    Guides discussion, asks questions
                                      and records findings
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

Do not invite people only to make the meeting look important. Every
participant should have a reason to be there.

## 4.4 Planning a workshop

### Step 1: Define the objective

The objective must explain what the session should achieve.

Weak objective:

> "Discuss the admission portal."

Better objective:

> "Develop a shared understanding of the current admission process,
> identify major delays and exceptions, and record unresolved
> questions."

### Step 2: Define the expected outputs

Examples: - Agreed list of current process steps. - List of known
problems. - List of business rules requiring confirmation. - List of
stakeholder needs. - Decisions made. - Open questions and action owners.

### Step 3: Select participants

Identify stakeholders who know the process, use the process, support it
or can make relevant decisions.

### Step 4: Prepare the agenda

A simple 60-minute agenda could be:

  ------------------------------------------------------------------------
                          Time Activity              Expected result
  ---------------------------- --------------------- ---------------------
                      0--5 min Welcome and objective Everyone understands
                                                     the purpose

                     5--15 min Explain current       Shared starting point
                               process               

                    15--30 min Identify problems and List of issues
                               exceptions            

                    30--45 min Discuss requirements  Candidate needs and
                               and improvement needs ideas

                    45--55 min Review disagreements  Clarifications and
                               and open questions    actions

                    55--60 min Summarize and close   Confirmed outputs and
                                                     next steps

### Step 5: Prepare materials

Depending on the topic, prepare: - Process diagrams. - Existing forms or
policies. - Interview findings. - A whiteboard or shared digital
board. - A list of questions. - A method for recording decisions and
actions.

Do not assume that every participant has read the same background
material. Provide a short explanation at the beginning.

### Step 6: Send the invitation

Include: - Workshop purpose. - Date, time and duration. -
Participants. - Agenda. - Preparation expected. - Relevant documents or
links. - Any decisions the participants are expected to help make.

## 4.5 Facilitating the workshop

The BA may act as the facilitator.

A facilitator should: - Explain the objective and agenda. - Encourage
participation from all relevant people. - Keep the discussion focused. -
Ask neutral questions. - Clarify unclear statements. - Manage
disagreements respectfully. - Separate facts from opinions and proposed
solutions. - Record decisions and unresolved issues. - Summarize the
result before closing.

### Useful facilitator questions

-   "Can someone explain how this step works today?"
-   "Does this process work the same way in every department?"
-   "What happens when the required document is missing?"
-   "Would anyone like to add a different perspective?"
-   "Are we discussing the current process or a proposed future
    process?"
-   "What evidence supports this rule?"
-   "Can we agree on this point, or should we record it as unresolved?"

# 5. Brainstorming

## 5.1 What is brainstorming?

Brainstorming is a group or individual technique used to generate a
range of ideas around a question or problem.

The main goal is to produce ideas before evaluating them. During idea
generation, participants should not reject every suggestion immediately.

**Example:** The college wants to understand why admission applications
are delayed. The group generates possible causes: - Missing documents. -
Unclear instructions. - Payment confirmation delays. - Manual data
entry. - Staff workload. - Repeated student follow-ups.

These are possible causes, not confirmed facts. They must be
investigated.

## 5.2 When should brainstorming be used?

Use brainstorming when: - The problem has several possible causes. - The
team needs improvement ideas. - Stakeholders have different
experiences. - The BA wants to explore options before narrowing them
down. - The team needs to identify possible exceptions or risks.

Brainstorming is not a substitute for evidence. An idea generated in a
session does not become a fact or requirement automatically.

## 5.3 Basic brainstorming rules

1.  **Define one clear question.** Everyone should understand what the
    group is trying to explore.
2.  **Encourage quantity first.** Generate several ideas before judging
    them.
3.  **Avoid early criticism.** Do not reject ideas while the group is
    still generating them.
4.  **Welcome different perspectives.** Unusual ideas may lead to useful
    discoveries.
5.  **Build on other ideas.** Participants may combine or improve
    suggestions.
6.  **Record every relevant idea.** Do not rely on memory.
7.  **Evaluate separately.** After idea generation, review ideas using
    agreed criteria.

## 5.4 A simple brainstorming session

**Problem question:**

"What could cause delays in the student admission process?"

### Stage 1: Generate ideas

Participants suggest: - Students upload incorrect documents. - Required
documents are not clearly explained. - Staff manually enter application
information. - Payment confirmation is delayed. - Applications wait for
manager approval. - Students do not know which documents are missing. -
The same information is checked more than once.

### Stage 2: Record without judging

Write every relevant suggestion on a board or in a shared document. At
this stage, do not label an idea as correct or incorrect.

### Stage 3: Group related ideas

This is called affinity grouping.

## 5.5 Brainstorming versus decision-making

These are different activities.

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Brainstorming                       Decision-making
  ----------------------------------- -----------------------------------
  Generates possibilities             Selects an option

  Encourages many ideas               Compares options against criteria

  Avoids early criticism              Requires evaluation and discussion

  Can include unusual suggestions     Considers feasibility, risk and
                                      value

  Output is a list of ideas           Output is a decision or
                                      recommendation
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

Do not tell stakeholders that an idea is approved merely because it was
suggested during brainstorming.

# 6. Observation

## 6.1 What is observation?

Observation is an elicitation technique in which the BA watches people,
activities, systems and interactions to understand how work is actually
performed.

Interviews tell the BA what people say they do. Observation can reveal
what happens in practice.

**Example:** An admissions officer explains that document verification
takes five minutes. The BA observes the process and notices that the
officer must open several files, check a spreadsheet and send a separate
email. The actual process may contain more steps than the interview
description revealed.

Observation should be respectful, authorized and conducted according to
organizational privacy and security rules.

## 6.2 When should a BA use observation?

Use observation when: - The BA needs to understand a real operational
process. - Stakeholders find it difficult to explain every step. - Work
includes repeated manual actions. - There may be unofficial
workarounds. - The BA needs to identify waiting time, handoffs or
interruptions. - The documented process may differ from actual
practice. - The BA needs to understand how a system is used in its real
working environment.

Observation may not reveal why someone performs an action. The BA should
ask follow-up questions at an appropriate time.

## 6.3 Types of observation

### A. Direct observation

The BA watches the activity as it occurs.

**Example:** Watching an admissions officer verify a student
application.

### B. Participant observation

The observer takes part in the activity, where permitted, to understand
it from the user's perspective.

**Example:** The BA follows an approved training workflow and performs
the steps under supervision.

Participant observation must not interfere with real operations or
involve unauthorized access.

### C. Non-participant observation

The observer watches without performing the task.

**Example:** The BA watches an officer process applications and records
the steps.

### D. Structured observation

The BA uses a prepared checklist or observation sheet.

**Example:** Record start time, end time, activity, system used and any
interruption for each process step.

### E. Unstructured observation

The BA watches more openly and records relevant behavior without a fixed
checklist.

This can be useful early in discovery when the BA does not yet know
which details matter.

## 6.4 Preparing for observation

Before observing:

1.  Define the objective.
2.  Select the process, role or activity to observe.
3.  Obtain the required permission.
4.  Agree on the time and location.
5.  Explain the purpose to the people being observed.
6.  Prepare an observation sheet.
7.  Decide what information is relevant.
8.  Follow privacy, confidentiality and security rules.

Do not collect personal or sensitive information unless it is necessary,
authorized and handled appropriately. Use sample or masked records
whenever possible.

## 6.7 Measuring time carefully

Suppose the BA observes the following activity:

  Activity                           Observed duration
  -------------------------------- -------------------
  Open application                            1 minute
  Check documents                            4 minutes
  Update tracking sheet                      2 minutes
  Send clarification email                   3 minutes
  Update application status                   1 minute
  **Total observed active time**        **11 minutes**

The total is 11 minutes for this observed case. It should not
automatically be reported as the average time for all applications.
Observe multiple representative cases and consider differences in
complexity.

Also distinguish: - **Active processing time:** Time spent doing the
task. - **Waiting time:** Time an item waits for a person, system or
external response. - **Elapsed time:** Total time from the start of a
process to its completion.

These measures answer different questions.

## 6.8 Observation etiquette and limitations

-   Do not interrupt the work unnecessarily.
-   Explain why you are observing.
-   Avoid judging the employee.
-   Record what you see, not what you assume.
-   Do not record passwords or confidential information.
-   Do not assume one observed case represents every case.
-   Ask about unusual actions after the activity, if appropriate.
-   Consider that people may change their behavior when they know they
    are being observed.

# 7. Document Analysis

## 7.1 What is document analysis?

Document analysis is the systematic review of existing documents,
records and artifacts to understand business processes, rules,
information, roles and requirements.

Documents may describe the official process, provide evidence of actual
activity or show information that a current system stores.

**Example:** The BA reviews: - Admission policy. - Student application
form. - Document checklist. - Fee schedule. - Application status
report. - Student complaint records. - Existing portal screens.

## 7.2 When should a BA use document analysis?

Use document analysis when: - The organization already has policies or
procedures. - The BA needs to understand business rules. - Existing
forms show what information is collected. - Reports can reveal volumes
or recurring problems. - The current system has fields, statuses or
workflows that need investigation. - The BA needs background information
before interviews or workshops. - The BA wants to compare official
procedures with actual practice.

## 7.3 Types of documents and what they reveal

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Document or artifact                What it can reveal
  ----------------------------------- -----------------------------------
  Business policy                     Mandatory rules and organizational
                                      direction

  Standard operating procedure (SOP)  Official steps for performing work

  Application form                    Information collected from users

  Checklist                           Items that must be verified

  Business rule document              Conditions, limits and decision
                                      rules

  Report                              Volumes, trends, errors or
                                      performance

  Spreadsheet                         Manual tracking, calculations and
                                      workarounds

  Existing system screen              Fields, options, statuses and user
                                      interactions

  Email or support ticket             Questions, complaints and recurring
                                      issues

  Contract or agreement               Obligations and conditions that may
                                      affect the solution

  Data dictionary                     Meaning, format and allowed values
                                      of data fields
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

A document may be outdated or incomplete. The BA must check its owner,
date, status and relevance.

## 7.4 How to analyze a document

### Step 1: Identify the document

Record its title, owner, version, date and purpose.

### Step 2: Check whether it is current

Ask: - Is this the latest approved version? - Who owns it? - Is it still
used? - Has a newer version replaced it?

### Step 3: Understand its purpose

Determine why the document exists and which process or decision it
supports.

### Step 4: Extract relevant information

Look for: - Process steps. - Roles and responsibilities. - Business
rules. - Required data. - Approval conditions. - Exceptions. -
Deadlines. - Statuses. - Reports and outputs.

### Step 5: Identify gaps and ambiguity

Look for: - Missing steps. - Undefined terms. - Conflicting rules. -
Unclear ownership. - Outdated references. - Different versions of the
same form. - Rules that do not explain exceptions.

### Step 6: Compare with other sources

Check the document against interviews, workshops, observation and other
records.

### Step 7: Record findings and questions

Do not silently resolve contradictions. Record them for clarification.

## 8.1 Triangulation

Triangulation means comparing information from multiple sources or
techniques to understand whether a finding is supported.

**Example finding:** Students do not know which documents are missing.

The BA checks:

-   **Interview:** Students report that they receive unclear messages.
-   **Questionnaire:** Several respondents say they cannot identify
    missing documents.
-   **Observation:** Staff manually send clarification emails.
-   **Document analysis:** The current policy lists mandatory documents
    but does not describe how missing items are communicated.

These sources point toward a communication issue. The BA should still
clarify the exact requirements, exceptions and business rules before
documenting a final solution requirement.

## 8.2 Handling conflicting information

Suppose: - The policy says every application requires manager
approval. - An officer says some applications are approved
automatically. - Observation shows the officer forwarding only selected
applications to a manager.

Do not choose one statement without investigation.

The BA should: 1. Record each source and its statement. 2. Check
document version and approval status. 3. Ask the officer for examples
and conditions. 4. Ask the policy owner whether an exception exists. 5.
Determine whether the difference is a formal rule, an informal
workaround or outdated documentation. 6. Record the confirmed rule and
any remaining uncertainty.

# 11. Interview Questions and Answers

## Q1. What is a requirements workshop?

**Answer:**

A requirements workshop is a structured group session where relevant
stakeholders collaborate to discuss business needs, processes, problems
and requirements. It has a defined objective, agenda, facilitator and
recorded outcomes.

## Q2. What is the role of a BA in a workshop?

**Answer:**

The BA may act as facilitator. The BA explains the objective, guides the
discussion, encourages participation, asks clarifying questions, manages
disagreements, records decisions and open questions, and summarizes the
outcomes.

## Q3. How do you prepare for a workshop?

**Answer:**

I define the objective and expected outputs, identify relevant
participants, prepare an agenda, review background information, arrange
materials and send an invitation with the purpose and preparation
details.

## Q4. What would you do if one participant dominates the workshop?

**Answer:**

I would acknowledge their contribution and invite other participants to
share their views. I may ask each person to contribute in turn or use
silent idea-writing so that quieter participants can provide input.

## Q5. What is brainstorming?

**Answer:**

Brainstorming is a technique used to generate many possible ideas around
a defined question or problem before evaluating them. It encourages
different perspectives and helps the team explore possible causes or
improvements.

## Q6. Why should ideas not be criticized during the initial brainstorming stage?

**Answer:**

Early criticism can discourage participation and reduce the number of
ideas generated. It is more effective to separate idea generation from
evaluation, then assess the ideas using agreed criteria.

## Q7. What is affinity grouping?

**Answer:**

Affinity grouping is the process of organizing related ideas into
categories. For example, ideas about missing documents, incorrect
uploads and unclear document lists may be grouped under "Document
problems."

## Q8. What is observation in business analysis?

**Answer:**

Observation is an elicitation technique where the BA watches people
perform their work to understand the actual process, tools, handoffs,
delays, exceptions and workarounds.

## Q9. What is the difference between participant and non-participant observation?

**Answer:**

In participant observation, the observer takes part in the activity
where permitted. In non-participant observation, the observer watches
without performing the task.

## Q10. What is document analysis?

**Answer:**

Document analysis is the systematic review of existing documents and
artifacts to understand business rules, process steps, roles, data,
requirements and current practices.

## Q11. What documents might a BA review?

**Answer:**

A BA may review policies, standard operating procedures, application
forms, checklists, business rule documents, reports, spreadsheets,
existing system screens, support tickets and data dictionaries.

## Q12. How do you know whether a document is reliable?

**Answer:**

I check its owner, version, date, approval status, purpose and whether
it is still used. I also compare important information with stakeholders
and other sources.

## Q13. What is triangulation?

**Answer:**

Triangulation is comparing information from multiple sources or
techniques to understand whether a finding is supported. For example,
the BA may compare interview statements with observation records and
written policies.

## Q14. What would you do if the documented process differs from the process you observe?

**Answer:**

I would record both versions, check whether the document is current and
ask the process owner and employees to explain the difference. It may be
an outdated document, an approved exception or an informal workaround. I
would not assume which version is correct without confirmation.

## Q15. Are brainstorming ideas automatically requirements?

**Answer:**

No. Brainstorming produces possible ideas. The BA must investigate their
business value, feasibility, relevance and stakeholder need before they
become agreed requirements.
