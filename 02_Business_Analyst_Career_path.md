# TechPhile.ai — Business Analyst Career Pathway

**Duration:** 45 learning days  
**Daily commitment:** 2–3 hours  
**Target audience:** Freshers and candidates with 0–1 year of experience  
**Pathway goal:** Teach students to understand business problems, gather requirements, analyze processes, document solutions and communicate with stakeholders.

## Start here: what this pathway is (read this first)

**In one sentence:** In 45 days you learn how a Business Analyst understands a business problem, gathers requirements, maps processes, uses data and works with a project team, and you finish with a complete BA portfolio project.

**Who is it for?** College students, fresh graduates and anyone with 0–1 year of experience. You need no technical or business background. If you can read, write clearly and use a computer, you can start on Day 1.

**What does a Business Analyst do?**
- Talks to the people who need a solution (stakeholders) to understand what the business really needs.
- Writes clear requirements, user stories and use cases that developers and testers can follow.
- Draws how work flows today and how it could be better (flowcharts and BPMN).
- Uses Excel and SQL to check facts and support decisions with data.
- Works in Agile teams with Scrum, Kanban and Jira, and checks the final solution with users (UAT).

**Jobs you can apply for afterwards:** Junior Business Analyst, Functional Analyst, Business Systems Analyst Trainee, Associate Product Analyst.

**What you need:** a laptop or PC with internet. All tools are free or have a free version, and each one is explained with sign-up or installation steps on the day you first use it, so do not install anything in advance. You will use Excel (or Google Sheets), a database tool for SQL, Jira, Figma and a free diagramming tool for flowcharts and BPMN.

**How each day works** (about 2–3 hours; heavy tool days can take up to 4):
1. **Read** the day's notes. They explain every new word and tool from zero, with pictures.
2. **Follow along:** repeat the worked example yourself in the tool.
3. **Try it:** do the small practice tasks, then compare with the answers.
4. **Build** the day's mini-project in the project workspace.
5. **Check:** run the checker or use the checklist, fix what it reports and compare with the expected results.
6. **Revise:** take the quiz and practise the interview questions.

**No trainer or mentor is needed.** Everything required to finish a day is inside that day's notes and project. If something is not explained, the notes are at fault, not you.

**By the end you will be able to:**
- Define a business problem, list stakeholders and write a project scope.
- Gather, write, prioritize and trace requirements, including user stories with acceptance criteria.
- Model a process as AS-IS and TO-BE and find root causes.
- Use Excel, SQL and basic statistics to support a recommendation.
- Run a sprint backlog in Jira and write UAT test cases.
- Present a complete business analysis pack to stakeholders.

## 1. Role overview

This pathway prepares a learner for entry-level work as a **Business Analyst**. Learners should be able to explain the concepts, complete practical tasks, validate their work and communicate technical decisions in a business context.

### Learning expectations

- Read the day's notes from top to bottom. They teach everything from zero.
- Repeat every worked example yourself in the tool (follow-along).
- Do the "Try it" tasks first, then compare with the answers shown afterwards.
- Complete the day's independent mini-project; do not substitute the final capstone.
- Run the project checker, read its messages, fix what it reports, then explain the result and its limitations in your own words.
- Keep your project files organized and submit the evidence listed for the day.

## 5. Daily lesson delivery specification

Use the following structure for **each day** when converting this roadmap into a full lesson manuscript. Keep the approved topic and project title unchanged.

### 5.1 Day information
- Day number and approved topic.
- Phase and module.
- Estimated time: 2–3 hours.
- Difficulty: assign Beginner, Intermediate or Advanced based on the actual prerequisite load.
- Prerequisites: list only previously taught concepts or clearly stated external setup requirements.

### 5.2 Learning objectives
Write 3–6 measurable outcomes using observable verbs such as define, distinguish, configure, implement, calculate, test, interpret or explain.

### 5.3 Self-study lesson notes
Write for a learner who has never seen the topic and has no trainer, mentor or AI helper. Never assume a term is known unless an earlier day taught it. Use these parts, in this order:
1. **Why this matters:** a short real job scenario.
2. **Words you need:** plain-English definitions of every new term, each with an example.
3. **The idea step by step:** short sections, each with a diagram, table or picture.
4. **Tool tour** (whenever a tool appears for the first time): an annotated screenshot with numbered labels, and a table saying what each numbered part is, where to find it and when to use it. Include installation or sign-up steps and what to do if they fail.
5. **Follow along:** click-by-click or line-by-line steps, with the expected result shown after each important step.
6. **Worked example:** one complete example with every number or output shown.
7. **Try it:** small practice tasks; show the answers only after the attempt.
8. **Common mistakes and error messages,** each with its fix.
9. **Interview-relevant points** and a short revision sheet.
10. **Bridge to the project:** which skills from the notes the project uses.

For code, explain every important line and show the expected output. For cloud work, include security, verification and safe cleanup. For data and ML work, include assumptions, evaluation and business interpretation. Mark days that need extra time as **Heavy day** (up to 4 hours).

### 5.4 Industry scenario
Describe a realistic business or engineering context, the problem, why this day's concept is needed and the expected practical outcome. Avoid asserting an unverified cause.

### 5.5 Independent mini-project
**Approved title:** Use the exact title in the day-wise syllabus table above.

Document the project with:
1. Problem statement and objective.
2. Skills practiced and prerequisites.
3. Functional requirements.
4. Inputs and expected outputs.
5. Implementation plan and ordered tasks.
6. The exact files in the learner's workspace: which to edit and submit, which are read-only input data, and which is the checker. Use one clear, flat file layout and name every file the way the workspace shows it.
7. Example input data or configuration.
8. Expected output/evidence.
9. Test cases and validation rules.
10. Common errors and debugging hints.
11. Business/technical interpretation.
12. Submission criteria and optional extension.

Keep the project independent and achievable in approximately 30–75 minutes. Do not provide the full solution before the learner has attempted the task.

### 5.6 Self-check and "if you are stuck" guide
Learners work alone, so every project must carry its own help:
- **Expected results:** a table or sample output the learner can compare with, shown in the project after the tasks.
- **Checker:** an automated check whose messages say exactly what is wrong and how to fix it, in plain language. It never only says "failed".
- **Stuck ladder,** written into the project from weakest to strongest help:
  1. Re-read the named section of the day's notes.
  2. Read the hint for the task (one small clue).
  3. Compare with the worked example in the notes.
  4. Read the common-errors table.
  5. Reveal the solution only after a real attempt, then write down what you missed.
- **Review checklist:** correctness, clear naming, validation, limits and interpretation, so learners can review their own work.

Any optional AI assistant on the platform is an extra. A day must be fully completable without it.

### 5.7 Daily quiz
Create five day-specific multiple-choice questions. Each must have four options (A–D), exactly one correct answer, and a clear explanation. Mix conceptual, practical, scenario and code/configuration interpretation questions as appropriate. Do not reuse question wording or test the same idea across adjacent days.

### 5.8 Interview preparation
Include at least two day-specific interview questions. For each, provide the correct answer, explanation, practical example where relevant and one follow-up question that probes deeper understanding.

### 5.9 Daily assessment and completion
- Concept completion: learner can explain the day's key ideas.
- Practical completion: approved mini-project runs or its non-code artifact is complete.
- Validation: required tests/checks pass and limitations are documented.
- Quiz: learner completes the five questions and reviews incorrect answers.
- Interview: learner answers at least two questions verbally or in writing.
- Evidence: learner submits source files, screenshots, report or architecture artifact as appropriate.
- Day complete: all required evidence is present and the learner can explain the result.

### 5.10 Visual and screenshot standard
- Use a picture wherever a learner must find something on screen or understand a flow: tool windows, menus, buttons, file layouts, diagrams, charts and expected results.
- Tool screenshots must be real, captured by the TechPhile team from the actual tool, with numbered labels, red boxes and arrows, and a table explaining each number. Do not use drawn imitations of a tool's interface.
- Screenshots must not show personal names, accounts, email addresses, notifications, taskbars or any third party's branding.
- Concept diagrams (lifecycles, flows, comparisons) are drawn once and reused. Every image needs a caption and alternative text.
- Use the same label style on every day so learners recognise it.

## 6. Phase assessments

At the end of every approved phase, create a cumulative assessment using only concepts already taught in that phase. Each assessment must include:

- Scenario and business/technical context.
- Requirements and constraints.
- Tasks and expected outputs.
- Evaluation rubric totaling 100%.
- Common mistakes and validation checks.
- At least five phase-relevant interview questions.

| Assessment dimension | Suggested weight |
|---|---:|
| Conceptual understanding | 20% |
| Practical correctness | 30% |
| Problem-solving and reasoning | 20% |
| Quality, organization and maintainability | 15% |
| Testing, validation and interpretation | 10% |
| Communication and evidence | 5% |
| **Total** | **100%** |

## 7. Final capstone project

Customer Onboarding and Service Improvement System
The student analyzes a company's customer onboarding process, identifies delays, documents requirements, creates improved workflows and prepares a prototype and UAT plan.
Deliverables:
- Business requirements document.
- Stakeholder analysis.
- User stories and acceptance criteria.
- AS-IS and TO-BE BPMN diagrams.
- Gap analysis.
- Wireframes.
- UAT test cases.
- Final stakeholder presentation.

### Capstone documentation checklist
- [ ] Business/industry scenario and problem statement.
- [ ] Business and technical objectives.
- [ ] Functional and non-functional requirements.
- [ ] Scope, assumptions and limitations.
- [ ] Technology stack and architecture.
- [ ] Complete folder structure and module breakdown.
- [ ] Database design and relationships, where applicable.
- [ ] Development phases and implementation sequence.
- [ ] Testing strategy and test cases.
- [ ] Security, privacy and access considerations.
- [ ] Deployment approach and verification.
- [ ] Expected outputs and documentation.
- [ ] Business interpretation and final presentation.
- [ ] Evaluation rubric and portfolio discussion points.

## 8. Portfolio expectations

A portfolio submission should include a clear README, problem statement, setup instructions, organized source files, sample or synthetic data where appropriate, validation evidence, screenshots or output samples, known limitations and a concise explanation of the learner's own contribution. Never include credentials, private keys, customer personal data or other secrets.

## 9. Career-readiness checklist

- [ ] Explain the role and typical entry-level responsibilities.
- [ ] Explain the core concepts in plain language.
- [ ] Complete practical tasks without copying a full reference solution.
- [ ] Debug common errors systematically.
- [ ] Validate outputs and state assumptions/limitations.
- [ ] Explain design choices and trade-offs.
- [ ] Present the final capstone and answer follow-up questions.
- [ ] Identify next skills that require additional practice.
