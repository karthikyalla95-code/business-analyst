## 1. Why teams use Agile

Imagine a company wants to launch an online appointment-booking system. A traditional project plan might attempt to define every requirement, screen and report before development begins. But after users try an early version, they may discover that rescheduling is more important than a feature the team planned first.

Agile is an approach to product development that emphasizes delivering useful outcomes in small increments, learning from feedback and adapting plans as understanding changes.

Instead of waiting until the entire system is complete, a team can deliver a small working capability, such as searching for an available appointment, collect feedback and then improve the next increment.

**Agile is not simply “working without a plan.”** Agile teams still plan, document, estimate, test and manage risk. They use feedback and evidence to adjust the plan as they learn.

### 1.1 What Agile helps a team do

- Deliver useful parts of a solution earlier.
- Get feedback from users and stakeholders regularly.
- Respond when business priorities change.
- Make work visible and discuss blockers.
- Improve quality through continuous testing and collaboration.
- Reduce the risk of building a large solution that users do not need.

Agile does not guarantee faster delivery or success. It depends on empowered people, stakeholder access, clear priorities, engineering quality and realistic expectations.

## 2. Agile vs plan-driven delivery

A plan-driven approach is often useful when scope and constraints are stable, work must follow fixed approvals, or a regulated process requires extensive upfront planning. Agile is useful when requirements are likely to evolve and frequent feedback can improve the solution. Many organizations use a hybrid approach.

| Dimension | Plan-driven approach | Agile approach |
|---|---|---|
| Planning | More detailed planning upfront | Initial direction plus ongoing replanning |
| Requirements | Often baselined early | Refined as the team learns |
| Delivery | Often organized around major phases/releases | Frequent usable increments |
| Feedback | Often at milestones | Regular throughout delivery |
| Change | Managed through formal change control | Expected and assessed continuously |
| Testing | May be concentrated in later phases | Integrated throughout development |
| Documentation | Often extensive and phase-based | Useful documentation created as needed |
| Success focus | Deliver agreed scope to plan | Deliver valuable outcomes and learn |

**Important:** Agile does not mean no documentation, no deadlines, no design or no governance. The goal is to create the documentation and controls that help the team deliver safely and effectively.

## 3. The Agile Manifesto

In 2001, the Agile Manifesto described four values:

1. **Individuals and interactions over processes and tools.**
2. **Working software over comprehensive documentation.**
3. **Customer collaboration over contract negotiation.**
4. **Responding to change over following a plan.**

The words on the right still have value. The manifesto says the items on the left are valued more when making trade-offs.

### 3.1 What these values mean in a BA's work

| Agile value | Practical BA behavior |
|---|---|
| Individuals and interactions | Talk directly with users, developers, testers and product decision-makers |
| Working software | Help the team validate requirements against a working increment |
| Customer collaboration | Invite stakeholder feedback during refinement and reviews |
| Responding to change | Reassess priorities when evidence or business needs change |

### 3.2 Twelve principles — practical interpretation

1. Satisfy customers through early and continuous delivery of valuable software.
2. Welcome changing requirements, even late in development, while assessing their impact responsibly.
3. Deliver working software frequently, with a preference for shorter timescales.
4. Business people and developers collaborate throughout the project.
5. Build work around motivated people and provide the support and trust they need.
6. Direct conversation is an effective way to communicate within a team.
7. Working software is an important measure of progress.
8. Maintain a sustainable pace rather than relying on constant overtime.
9. Pay continuous attention to technical excellence and good design.
10. Keep work simple by maximizing the amount of work not done.
11. Good solutions emerge from teams that organize their work effectively.
12. Regularly reflect on how to become more effective, then adjust behavior.

A BA supports these principles by making business intent clear, involving stakeholders, keeping requirements understandable and helping the team learn from feedback.

## 4. Agile frameworks and methods

Agile is a set of values and principles, not one mandatory process. Several methods and frameworks apply Agile ideas differently.

| Method/framework | Main idea | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Scrum | Work in fixed-length Sprints with defined accountabilities, events and artifacts | Product development with regular inspection and adaptation |
| Kanban | Visualize work, limit work in progress and improve flow | Support, operations and continuous delivery |
| Extreme Programming (XP) | Engineering practices such as pair programming, continuous integration and test-driven development | Software teams emphasizing technical quality |
| Lean | Improve customer value and reduce waste | Process and product improvement |

This lesson focuses on Scrum. Kanban is introduced for comparison; it is covered in more depth later if included in the pathway.

## 5. Scrum framework

Scrum is a lightweight framework for generating value through adaptive solutions to complex problems. It uses a small team, a Product Goal, a Product Backlog, Sprints and regular inspection and adaptation.

A Sprint is a fixed-length event of one month or less. A team selects work for a Sprint, creates an Increment that meets the Definition of Done and inspects the outcome with stakeholders.

### 5.1 Scrum Team accountabilities

The Scrum Team has three accountabilities:

**Product Owner**
- Accountable for maximizing the value of the product resulting from the Scrum Team's work.
- Develops and communicates the Product Goal.
- Creates and clearly communicates Product Backlog items.
- Orders Product Backlog items.
- Ensures the Product Backlog is transparent, visible and understood.

**Scrum Master**
- Accountable for establishing Scrum as defined in the Scrum Guide.
- Helps the team and organization understand Scrum theory and practice.
- Coaches the team toward effectiveness and self-management.
- Helps remove impediments and improve the team's ways of working.

**Developers**
- Create a usable Increment every Sprint.
- Create the Sprint Backlog plan.
- Adapt their plan each day toward the Sprint Goal.
- Hold each other accountable as professionals.
- Ensure the Increment meets the Definition of Done.

Developers are not only programmers. The term includes the people who create any usable aspect of the Increment, including analysis, design, testing and other skills needed.

### 5.2 Where does the Business Analyst fit?

The Scrum Guide does not define a separate Business Analyst accountability. A BA may work as part of the Scrum Team, support the Product Owner, or work across teams depending on the organization.

Typical BA contributions include:

- Explore business problems and desired outcomes with stakeholders.
- Help clarify users, business rules, constraints and assumptions.
- Support Product Backlog refinement by making items understandable.
- Draft user stories and acceptance criteria when useful.
- Model workflows and explain current and proposed processes.
- Identify dependencies, data needs, exceptions and impacts.
- Collaborate with Developers and testers to resolve questions.
- Help prepare stakeholder feedback and assess whether the Increment meets the business need.
- Maintain traceability or regulatory documentation where required.

A BA does not automatically own the Product Backlog, assign work to Developers or decide technical implementation. Those responsibilities should be clear in the team's operating model.

## 6. Scrum artifacts and commitments

Scrum defines three artifacts. Each has a commitment that helps provide transparency and focus.

| Artifact | What it contains | Commitment |
|---|---|---|
| Product Backlog | Ordered, evolving list of what is needed to improve the product | Product Goal |
| Sprint Backlog | Sprint Goal, selected Product Backlog items and actionable plan | Sprint Goal |
| Increment | Usable, completed work that adds to previous Increments | Definition of Done |

### 6.1 Product Backlog

The Product Backlog is the single, emergent and ordered list of work needed to improve the product. It is never considered permanently complete.

Items may include:
- New features.
- Defects and reliability improvements.
- Technical work.
- Research or discovery.
- Security, compliance and performance needs.

The Product Owner is accountable for effective Product Backlog management. The BA can help clarify and refine items, but should not confuse supporting the backlog with owning its ordering.

### 6.2 Sprint Backlog

The Sprint Backlog contains:
1. The Sprint Goal — why the Sprint is valuable.
2. The Product Backlog items selected for the Sprint.
3. The plan for delivering the Increment.

It is created by and for the Developers. It is updated as the team learns more during the Sprint.

### 6.3 Increment and Definition of Done

An Increment is a concrete stepping stone toward the Product Goal. It must be usable and meet the Definition of Done.

A Definition of Done is a formal description of the quality measures required for a product Increment. It creates a shared understanding of what “done” means.

Example Definition of Done for an appointment-booking feature:
- Code is reviewed and merged.
- Required automated tests pass.
- Booking rules and validation are implemented.
- No unresolved critical defect remains.
- Accessibility and security checks required by the team are completed.
- Product documentation is updated where needed.
- The feature is integrated and usable with the existing product.

Acceptance criteria for an individual backlog item and the Definition of Done are related but different. Acceptance criteria describe conditions for a particular item; the Definition of Done applies to the quality of the Increment.

## 7. Scrum events and how a BA contributes

Scrum has five events: the Sprint, Sprint Planning, Daily Scrum, Sprint Review and Sprint Retrospective. The Sprint contains the other events.

### 7.1 The Sprint

The Sprint is the container for all other Scrum events. It is one month or less. A new Sprint starts immediately after the previous one concludes.

During a Sprint:
- The team works toward the Sprint Goal.
- Quality does not decrease.
- The Product Backlog is refined as needed.
- Scope may be clarified and renegotiated with the Product Owner as more is learned, without endangering the Sprint Goal.

### 7.2 Sprint Planning

**Purpose:** Establish why the Sprint is valuable, what can be done and how the work will be completed.

The Scrum Team discusses:
1. Why is this Sprint valuable? — Sprint Goal.
2. What can be Done this Sprint? — selected Product Backlog items.
3. How will the chosen work get done? — Developers' plan.

BA contribution:
- Clarify the business objective and expected value.
- Explain user needs, process rules and exceptions.
- Bring open questions and dependencies to the discussion.
- Help confirm that backlog items are understandable.
- Avoid committing the team to work on behalf of Developers.

### 7.3 Daily Scrum

A 15-minute event for Developers to inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapt the Sprint Backlog as necessary.

It is not intended to be a status report to a manager. A BA may attend when useful for collaboration, but should not turn it into a requirements meeting or interrupt the Developers' inspection.

### 7.4 Sprint Review

The Scrum Team and stakeholders inspect the outcome of the Sprint and discuss progress toward the Product Goal. They collaborate on what to do next.

BA contribution:
- Explain the business problem and intended outcome.
- Help stakeholders evaluate the Increment against real workflows.
- Capture feedback, new information and follow-up questions.
- Help turn relevant feedback into backlog items for Product Owner consideration.

A Sprint Review is not merely a slide presentation or a one-way demo.

### 7.5 Sprint Retrospective

The Scrum Team inspects how the last Sprint went with regard to individuals, interactions, processes, tools and the Definition of Done. It identifies useful improvements.

BA contribution:
- Share where requirement clarification helped or slowed delivery.
- Identify repeated misunderstandings or late changes.
- Suggest improvements to refinement, stakeholder access or documentation.
- Agree on a practical experiment with the team.

The retrospective is about improving the team's way of working, not blaming individuals.

### 7.6 Scrum event summary

| Event | Main question | Timebox |
|---|---|---|
| Sprint | What can we achieve in this fixed period? | One month or less |
| Sprint Planning | Why, what and how? | Up to 8 hours for a one-month Sprint |
| Daily Scrum | How are we progressing toward the Sprint Goal? | 15 minutes |
| Sprint Review | What did we achieve and what should happen next? | Up to 4 hours for a one-month Sprint |
| Sprint Retrospective | How can we improve quality and effectiveness? | Up to 3 hours for a one-month Sprint |

For shorter Sprints, events are usually shorter. The timeboxes above are maximums from the Scrum Guide for a one-month Sprint, not mandatory meeting durations for every team.

## 8. Product mindset: focus on outcomes, not just output

A feature is an output. The improvement it creates for a user or business is an outcome.

Example:
- Output: Add an appointment reminder notification.
- Intended outcome: Fewer missed appointments.
- Possible measure: No-show rate.
- Business impact: Better use of staff capacity and appointment slots.

A BA should ask:
- Which user problem are we solving?
- Who experiences the problem?
- What evidence shows the problem matters?
- What behavior or result should improve?
- How will we measure the improvement?
- What risks or unintended effects should we monitor?

### 8.1 Output vs outcome vs impact

| Level | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Output | What the team delivers | Reminder feature |
| Outcome | Change in user behavior or process | More users attend booked appointments |
| Impact | Wider business result | Better capacity utilization and lower avoidable cost |

Do not assume an output creates the intended outcome. Measure after release and learn.

## 9. Backlog refinement and prioritization

Product Backlog refinement is the ongoing activity of breaking down and further defining Product Backlog items, including adding details such as description, order and size. It is not a formal Scrum event and there is no prescribed duration.

A BA can help the team:
- Clarify the user and business problem.
- Identify business rules and alternate paths.
- Split large items into smaller valuable items.
- Add acceptance criteria.
- Identify dependencies and risks.
- Make assumptions explicit.
- Bring questions to the right stakeholders.

### 9.1 Example backlog

Scenario: An appointment-booking product.

| Order | Backlog item | User value | Notes |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 1 | View available appointment slots | Users can see real availability | Core booking journey |
| 2 | Book an available slot | Users can reserve a time | Prevent double booking |
| 3 | Receive booking confirmation | Users know booking succeeded | Email or in-app channel |
| 4 | Reschedule a booking | Users can change plans | Define cutoff rule |
| 5 | Cancel a booking | Users can release a slot | Define cancellation policy |
| 6 | View booking history | Users can review past bookings | Lower initial priority |

The Product Owner is accountable for ordering the Product Backlog. The BA can provide evidence, dependencies and impact analysis to support that decision.

### 9.2 MoSCoW prioritization

MoSCoW is one technique for classifying requirements:

- **Must have:** Essential for the agreed release or outcome.
- **Should have:** Important, but a workable alternative or workaround exists temporarily.
- **Could have:** Useful but lower priority.
- **Won't have this time:** Explicitly excluded from this delivery scope; it may be reconsidered later.

MoSCoW is not a Scrum rule. Use it as a conversation aid, define the scope/time context and avoid labeling every item a Must.

### 9.3 Relative estimation and story points

Some teams use story points to estimate relative effort, complexity and uncertainty. Story points are not hours, days or a measure of an individual's productivity.

For example, a team may estimate:
- View available slots: 2 points.
- Book a slot with conflict handling: 5 points.
- Build a complex reporting dashboard: 8 points.

These numbers are illustrative and meaningful only within that team's estimation approach. Do not compare story points across teams.

## 10. Writing Agile user stories

A common user story format is:

**As a [type of user], I want [capability], so that [benefit].**

Example:

> As a registered customer, I want to view available appointment slots so that I can choose a suitable time.

A good story communicates the user, need and value. It is not a complete specification by itself. The team discusses details and adds acceptance criteria.

### 10.1 Acceptance criteria using Given–When–Then

**Given** a registered customer is signed in  
**When** the customer opens the appointment page  
**Then** the system displays available appointment slots for the selected service and date.

Additional scenarios:
- Given no slots are available, when the customer searches, then the system explains that no slots are available and offers another date.
- Given a slot becomes unavailable before confirmation, when the customer tries to book it, then the system prevents duplicate booking and asks the customer to choose another slot.
- Given the selected date is in the past, when the customer searches, then the system does not offer past appointment slots.

Acceptance criteria should be specific, testable and understandable to business stakeholders and testers.

### 10.2 INVEST as a story quality check

| Letter | Meaning | Question |
|---|---|---|
| I | Independent | Can this story be developed with minimal dependency? |
| N | Negotiable | Can details be discussed rather than treated as a fixed contract? |
| V | Valuable | Does it deliver recognizable user or business value? |
| E | Estimable | Does the team understand enough to estimate it? |
| S | Small | Can it reasonably fit into a Sprint? |
| T | Testable | Can the team verify whether it is complete? |

INVEST is a helpful heuristic, not a mandatory Scrum rule.

## 11. Handling change during a Sprint

Agile welcomes new information, but a team should not casually disrupt its Sprint Goal every time someone requests a feature.

When a new request arrives:
1. Record the request and the problem it addresses.
2. Ask why it is urgent and who needs the change.
3. Clarify business value, risk, deadline and dependencies.
4. Discuss impact with the Product Owner and relevant Developers.
5. Decide whether it belongs in the current Sprint or should be ordered in the Product Backlog.
6. If scope needs to change, collaborate without endangering the Sprint Goal.
7. Record the decision and communicate it to affected stakeholders.

The Product Owner may cancel a Sprint if the Sprint Goal becomes obsolete. This is uncommon and should not be used as routine scope management.

## 12. Kanban: a complementary way to visualize flow

Kanban is a strategy for optimizing the flow of value through a process. A simple board makes work visible.

Example board:

| To Do | In Progress | Review | Done |
|---|---|---|---|
| Add booking search | Validate booking rules | Test confirmation email | Define appointment types |
| Add cancellation policy |  |  |  |

### 12.1 Work in progress limits

A Work In Progress (WIP) limit restricts how many items can be active in a workflow stage.

Example: If the Review column has a WIP limit of 2 and already contains 2 items, the team should help complete or unblock review work before pulling another item into that stage.

Benefits:
- Highlights bottlenecks.
- Encourages finishing work rather than starting too much.
- Makes delays visible.
- Supports smoother flow.

### 12.2 Scrum and Kanban are not identical

Scrum uses Sprints, defined accountabilities, events and artifacts. Kanban focuses on visualizing work, managing flow and improving the system. A Scrum Team can also use Kanban practices such as a board and WIP limits.

## 13. Practical mini-project — Build an Agile product backlog

**Estimated time:** 45–75 minutes  
**Scenario:** A local clinic wants a basic appointment-booking product. Patients currently call the clinic to ask about available times. Staff manually write appointments in a register, which can lead to duplicate bookings and missed confirmations.

**Goal:** Prepare a small, understandable Product Backlog that a Scrum Team could discuss and refine.

### 13.1 Business context

Stakeholders:
- Patients who book appointments.
- Reception staff who manage appointments.
- Doctors who provide appointment slots.
- Clinic manager who monitors service quality.

Business objectives:
- Reduce manual booking effort.
- Prevent double booking.
- Help patients receive clear confirmation.
- Make cancellations and rescheduling easier.

Constraints and assumptions:
- This is a learning exercise, not a real clinical system.
- Do not collect or store actual patient personal or medical information.
- The first release supports one clinic location.
- Appointment availability must be checked before a booking is confirmed.
- The clinic has not yet selected a notification provider.

### 13.2 Required deliverables

Create a folder:

├── product_goal.md
├── product_backlog.csv
├── acceptance_criteria.md

**Deliverable 1 — product_goal.md**

Write one concise Product Goal describing the user, problem and desired outcome.

**Deliverable 2 — product_backlog.csv**

Create a CSV with these columns:

~~~csv
ID,User_Story,Priority,Acceptance_Criteria_Summary,Dependencies,Open_Questions
~~~

Create at least 8 backlog items. Include:
- View available slots.
- Book a slot.
- Prevent double booking.
- Receive confirmation.
- Cancel an appointment.
- Reschedule an appointment.
- Staff view of daily appointments.
- Search appointment history.

Order the items based on user value, risk and dependencies. You may use MoSCoW labels, but explain the context for each Must.

**Deliverable 3 — acceptance_criteria.md**

**Deliverable 4 — sprint_plan.md**

Propose a one-week learning Sprint:
- A Sprint Goal.
- The selected backlog items.

- Dependencies and risks.
- A Definition of Done.
- How stakeholders will inspect the result at Sprint Review.

This is a practice plan. Do not pretend that the learner has completed software implementation.

### 13.3 Expected quality checks

Your work should satisfy these checks:

| Check | Expected result |
|---|---|
| Product Goal | One clear, outcome-oriented statement |
| Backlog size | At least 8 distinct items |
| User story format | User, capability and benefit are clear |
| Ordering | Highest-value and enabling work appears first, with rationale |
| Acceptance criteria | At least 3 stories have testable Given–When–Then scenarios |
| Alternate paths | Each of the 3 detailed stories includes a failure or alternate case |
| Dependencies | Important dependencies are recorded |
| Sprint Goal | States a coherent outcome, not just a list of tasks |
| Definition of Done | Describes quality checks for completed work |
| Open questions | Unknown policies and decisions are visible |

### 13.4 Sample expected story

**ID:** US-01  
**Story:** As a patient, I want to view available appointment slots so that I can choose a suitable time.  
**Priority:** Must for the first booking release, subject to stakeholder agreement.  
**Acceptance criteria summary:** Available slots are shown for a selected service and date; unavailable slots cannot be selected; no-results state explains what the user can do next.  
**Dependency:** Clinic schedule and slot rules must be defined.  
**Open question:** How long can a slot remain reserved while a patient completes booking?

## 14. Common Agile mistakes

| Mistake | Why it is a problem | Better approach |
|---|---|---|
| Calling Agile “no planning” | Teams still need goals, priorities and coordination | Plan at an appropriate level and adapt as evidence changes |
| Treating Scrum Master as project boss | Scrum Master serves the team and helps establish Scrum | Respect accountabilities and team self-management |
| Treating Product Owner as a requirements secretary | Product Owner is accountable for maximizing product value | Make value and ordering decisions explicit |
| Assuming BA is a formal Scrum accountability | Scrum defines Product Owner, Scrum Master and Developers | Clarify the BA's contribution in the organization |
| Treating Daily Scrum as manager status reporting | It reduces collaboration and adaptation | Inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal |
| Treating Sprint Review as a presentation only | Stakeholder feedback and product adaptation are missed | Inspect the Increment and discuss what to do next |
| Calling every backlog item a Must | Nothing is meaningfully prioritized | Make trade-offs and explain the release context |
| Writing technical tasks as user stories | User value is hidden | Describe the user need and benefit; add technical tasks where appropriate |
| Treating story points as hours | Estimation becomes misleading | Use relative estimates within the team |
| Changing Sprint Goal every day | Focus and predictability suffer | Assess changes with the Product Owner and Developers |
| Saying “done” when code is merely written | Quality and integration may be incomplete | Apply the Definition of Done |
| Measuring success by number of tickets closed | Activity does not prove user value | Measure outcomes and product impact |

## 15. Daily quiz

Choose one answer for each question. Review the explanation after answering.

**Q1. Which statement best describes Agile?**

A. A method where teams never create plans.  
B. An approach that delivers value incrementally and adapts using feedback.  
C. A method where all requirements must be fixed before work starts.  
D. A framework that removes the need for testing.

**Answer: B.** Agile emphasizes collaboration, useful increments, feedback and adaptation. It does not remove planning, documentation or testing.

**Q2. Who is accountable for maximizing the value of the product in Scrum?**

A. Scrum Master  
B. Developers  
C. Product Owner  
D. Project sponsor

**Answer: C.** The Product Owner is accountable for maximizing product value and effective Product Backlog management.

**Q3. What is the purpose of the Daily Scrum?**

A. To give a status report to a manager.  
B. To inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapt the Sprint Backlog.  
C. To approve every requirement change.  
D. To demonstrate the finished product to customers.

**Answer: B.** The Daily Scrum is a 15-minute event for Developers to inspect progress and adapt their plan.

**Q4. Which is the best example of an outcome rather than an output?**

A. A reminder notification feature is released.  
B. Five screens are designed.  
C. The number of source files increases.  
D. The appointment no-show rate decreases.

**Answer: D.** A lower no-show rate is a change in business or user results. The notification feature is an output that may or may not produce that outcome.

**Q5. Which statement about acceptance criteria and Definition of Done is correct?**

A. They always mean exactly the same thing.  
B. Acceptance criteria describe conditions for a particular backlog item; Definition of Done describes quality required for the Increment.  
C. Definition of Done is written separately for every user story and has no team-wide meaning.  
D. Neither is related to quality.

**Answer: B.** Acceptance criteria are item-specific. Definition of Done provides a shared quality standard for the Increment.

## 16. Interview preparation

### Q1. What is Agile, and how does it help a Business Analyst?

**Answer:** Agile is an approach to product development that emphasizes delivering value in small increments, collaborating with stakeholders and adapting based on feedback. A BA helps the team understand the business problem, clarify requirements, model workflows, refine backlog items and validate whether the delivered increment meets user needs. For example, in an appointment-booking product, I would clarify booking rules and alternate scenarios before the team implements the booking flow.

**Follow-up:** Does Agile mean documentation is unnecessary?

**Answer:** No. Agile values working software over comprehensive documentation, but useful documentation is still important. The team creates documentation that supports understanding, delivery, compliance and maintenance.

### Q2. Explain the three Scrum accountabilities.

**Answer:** The Product Owner is accountable for maximizing product value and effective Product Backlog management. The Scrum Master is accountable for establishing Scrum and helping the team and organization use it effectively. Developers create a usable Increment each Sprint and manage the plan for their work. The Scrum Team works together toward the Product Goal.

**Follow-up:** Is a Business Analyst one of the three formal Scrum accountabilities?

**Answer:** No. The Scrum Guide defines Product Owner, Scrum Master and Developers. A BA may contribute within the team or support the Product Owner, depending on the organization.

### Q3. What happens during Sprint Planning and Sprint Review?

**Answer:** In Sprint Planning, the Scrum Team discusses why the Sprint is valuable, what can be done and how the work will be completed. During Sprint Review, the team and stakeholders inspect the Sprint outcome and discuss what to do next. As a BA, I can clarify the business objective and help stakeholders give useful feedback.

**Follow-up:** Is Sprint Review only a product demo?

**Answer:** No. A demo may be part of it, but the event is a collaborative inspection of the outcome and the product's future direction.

### Q4. What makes a good Agile user story?

**Answer:** A good user story communicates who needs a capability, what they need and why it matters. It should be small enough to discuss, valuable, understandable and testable. I use INVEST as a quality check and add acceptance criteria to describe expected behavior, including important alternate cases.

**Follow-up:** What if the story is too large?

**Answer:** I would explore the user's journey and split the story into smaller, independently valuable slices while preserving the business outcome.

### Q5. How would you handle a new urgent requirement during a Sprint?

**Answer:** I would first understand the business reason, urgency, impact and dependencies. I would document the request and discuss it with the Product Owner and relevant Developers. The team would decide whether it can be addressed without endangering the Sprint Goal or whether it should be ordered in the Product Backlog for later. I would communicate the decision and its rationale to stakeholders.

**Follow-up:** Can the Product Owner change the Sprint Backlog directly?

**Answer:** The Sprint Backlog is a plan by and for the Developers. The Product Owner can discuss scope changes and negotiate with Developers, but should not unilaterally assign work or take over their plan.
